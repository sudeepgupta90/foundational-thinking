# The Socratic Method — Codified Principles

> "I am the kind of man who listens to nothing within me but the argument that on
> reflection seems best to me."
> — Plato, *Crito* 46b

## What it is

A method for destroying false confidence. Not a teaching technique, not a rhetorical
style, and not "asking a lot of questions." The Socratic method is an adversarial
procedure applied to *beliefs* — including, and especially, one's own — whose success
condition is the discovery that a confidently held claim cannot survive its own
implications.

Its product is usually not an answer. Its product is a question that is finally worth
asking.

## Provenance

The method is reconstructed from Plato's early ("Socratic") dialogues — *Euthyphro*,
*Laches*, *Charmides*, *Lysis*, *Meno*, *Gorgias*, *Protagoras* — and secondarily from
Xenophon's *Memorabilia*. The historical Socrates wrote nothing. What follows is the
procedure as it operates in those texts, not a biography.

The canonical modern analysis is Gregory Vlastos, "The Socratic Elenchus," *Oxford
Studies in Ancient Philosophy* 1 (1983), which first stated the elenchus as a formal
argument structure rather than a conversational habit.

## The seven commitments

### 1. Professed ignorance (*eironeia*)

The inquirer enters holding no thesis to defend.

> "I am wiser than this man; it is likely that neither of us knows anything worthwhile,
> but he thinks he knows something when he does not, whereas when I do not know, neither
> do I think I know." — *Apology* 21d

This is the enabling condition for everything else, and it is not modesty. An inquirer
with a thesis to protect will steer, and steering destroys the method: whatever the
interlocutor then concedes is a concession to *the inquirer*, not to the argument.

**Operationally:** you may not have a preferred answer while running an elenchus. If you
do, you are advocating. Advocacy is a legitimate activity — it is what disputation is for
— but it is a different activity, and running it while claiming to inquire is the failure
mode Plato names *sophistry*.

### 2. Elenchus — the engine

*Elenchos*: examination, testing, refutation. The formal structure:

1. The interlocutor asserts **p**, typically with confidence.
2. The inquirer secures sincere assent to further claims **q** and **r** — each of which
   the interlocutor accepts *independently*, on its own merits, without seeing where it
   leads.
3. The inquirer shows that **q ∧ r** entails **¬p**.
4. The interlocutor must give something up. **p** was not known.

The force of the elenchus comes entirely from step 2: the premises are the
interlocutor's own. Nothing has been imposed. This is why a refuted interlocutor cannot
retreat to "well, that's your opinion" — the contradiction was assembled from their
materials.

**What makes an elenchus fail:** premises supplied by the inquirer rather than extracted
from the interlocutor. A refutation built on the inquirer's premises refutes nothing; it
merely states a disagreement.

### 3. Definition-seeking — an example is not a definition

Asked what virtue is, Meno produces a list: the virtue of a man, of a woman, of a child,
of a slave.

> "I asked you for one virtue and you have given me a swarm of them."
> — *Meno* 72a-b

The demand is for what is common to every instance and absent from every non-instance —
the *eidos*, "that form itself by which all pious things are pious" (*Euthyphro* 6d-e).
Laches makes the identical mistake with courage, offering "standing firm in battle"
(*Laches* 190e-191a).

**Why this is the hinge:** almost every substantive dispute that appears to be about
facts is actually a dispute in which two parties are using one word for two things.
Producing examples feels like progress and reliably conceals this. Nothing can be
decided about X until it is settled what X is.

### 4. Aporia — the productive dead end

*Aporia*: impasse, "no way through." The dialogues characteristically end in it.
Euthyphro leaves without knowing what piety is. This is not a literary failure.

Meno complains that Socrates has numbed him like a torpedo fish (*Meno* 80a-b). Socrates'
answer, in the slave-boy demonstration, is direct: the boy who now knows he cannot solve
the problem is **better off** than the boy who wrongly thought he could —

> "he will be better off for having been numbed... now he would be glad to seek it,
> since he does not think he knows." — *Meno* 84a-c

**Aporia is the deliverable.** A confidently held false belief is worse than an
acknowledged gap, because the false belief forecloses inquiry and the gap invites it.
Reaching "we do not actually know what we are trying to do here" is a completed elenchus,
not an abandoned one.

**The counterpart failure:** aporia treated as a resting place. Numbness is a transition
state. Having dissolved a bad question, you owe a better one.

### 5. Maieutics — midwifery

> "I attend the men and not the women, and I practise on their souls when they are in
> labour... The greatest thing about my art is that it can test whether the young man's
> mind is bringing forth a phantom and a lie, or something real and true."
> — *Theaetetus* 150b-151d

The inquirer delivers; the inquirer does not conceive. Everything in the conclusion must
have come from the interlocutor.

**Operationally:** the distinction between a real question and a leading one. "Have you
considered that the cache might not be the problem?" is not a question — it is an
assertion wearing a question mark, and assent to it is assent to *you*. "What would we
expect to see in the logs if the cache were the cause?" is a question: it can be answered
either way and its answer is the interlocutor's own.

### 6. Follow the logos wherever it leads

The argument decides. Not the status of the arguer, not the consequences of the
conclusion, not how much work has already been done on the assumption that **p**.

Socrates accepts refutation cheerfully when it comes and holds his position under a death
sentence when it doesn't. The two behaviors are the same behavior: in both cases he is
tracking the argument and ignoring everything else.

**The two symmetric failures:** yielding to an interlocutor's authority when their
argument has not improved, and clinging to a position after its premises have been
conceded away.

### 7. Sincere assent, one question at a time

The interlocutor must say **what they actually believe**, not what is easy to defend.
Socrates repeatedly halts to enforce this against Callicles (*Gorgias* 495a, 500b) —
Vlastos calls it the "say what you believe" constraint.

A concession made to be agreeable is worthless: the contradiction it later produces is a
contradiction in the interlocutor's *diplomacy*, not in their beliefs, and refuting it
changes nothing.

Correspondingly: **one question at a time.** A batch of six questions is answered
strategically, as a set. A single question is answered honestly, because there is nothing
to position against.

## The question moves

The six-way taxonomy in common use — clarification, assumptions, evidence, implications,
alternative viewpoints, and questions about the question — is **Richard Paul and Linda
Elder's twentieth-century systematization** (*The Thinker's Guide to the Art of Socratic
Questioning*), not a classical scheme. It is a genuinely useful checklist. It is not
Plato, and it should not be presented as if it were.

| Move | Asks | Bites when |
|---|---|---|
| **Clarification** | What do you mean by that word? Can you give a case that is *not* an instance? | A term is doing heavy load-bearing work undefined |
| **Probing assumptions** | What has to be true for that to hold? What are you taking as given? | The premise is invisible because everyone shares it |
| **Probing evidence** | How do you know? What would we observe if it were false? | A conclusion is being reported as an observation |
| **Implications** | If that is so, what else follows? Are you willing to accept that? | The claim is plausible in isolation and unacceptable in consequence |
| **Alternative viewpoints** | What would someone who disagrees say? What else would explain this? | A single explanation arrived first and was never competed |
| **Meta** | Why does this question matter? Is this the question we should be asking? | The question itself is the thing that is wrong |

The most valuable move is usually the last one.

## Failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Why it is fatal |
|---|---|---|
| **Sophistry** | Questions engineered to win rather than to test | The inquirer had a thesis. The concessions are worthless. |
| **Leading questions** | Assertions with question marks; "Wouldn't you agree that…" | Violates maieutics — the conclusion is implanted, not delivered |
| **Interrogation** | Ten questions at once; questioning as obstruction | Answers become strategic; also, at some point you have to build the thing |
| **Infinite regress** | Every answer generates three more questions, forever | Definition-seeking has no natural terminus; the inquirer must impose one |
| **Aporia as destination** | "So we don't really know anything" and a full stop | Numbness without reconstruction is just demoralization |
| **Elenchus on the person** | Testing the interlocutor rather than the claim | The method examines beliefs. It is not a competence review. |

## Where this applies to agent work

The elenchus is a **premise-integrity check**, and premises are exactly what agentic work
tends to inherit without inspection. The characteristic pattern: a user states a goal or a
diagnosis, the agent adopts it as a specification, and every subsequent correct action
compounds an error made in the first sentence. Speed of compliance is what makes it
expensive — the agent is fast, so it gets a long way down the wrong road.

Three recurring shapes:

- **The unquantified goal** — "make it faster," "make it reliable." No success condition
  exists, so any output can be declared to satisfy it, and none of them can be shown to.
- **The asserted cause** — "the bug is X, go fix X." A hypothesis has been handed over
  with the grammar of an observation. Fixing X will also *appear* to work if the symptom
  is intermittent.
- **The smuggled frame** — "which of these should we delete?" presupposes deletion is the
  move. Answering the question ratifies the presupposition.

In each, the correct first act is the same: refuse to inherit the premise, and get it
stated as something that can be true or false.

## What this method cannot do

The elenchus is **dissolvent**. It establishes that a belief is unfounded; it does not
establish what to believe instead, and it does not decide anything. An inquirer who only
ever runs elenchus produces a team that knows its question is ill-formed and still has to
ship on Thursday.

When the question is finally well-formed and a decision must be made, the method you need
is the other one: see [scholastic-disputation.md](./scholastic-disputation.md).
